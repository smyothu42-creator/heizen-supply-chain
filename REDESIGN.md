# Discovery is three jobs, not one screen

*17 August 2026 · Sai, for Aman, Abhilasha and Jeet*

Everything the meeting raised comes back to the same thing. An introductory call, a
real discovery call and an account expansion are different jobs, and the tool has
been giving all three the same dossier.

Nearly every note was a version of one complaint: there is a lot of information being
shown everywhere, and none of it is arranged around what the consultant is about to
do. A first call needs six facts in fifteen minutes. A second call needs three of
those facts in depth and a question flow that goes where the last answer pointed. An
expansion conversation needs neither, because it is about what nobody has looked at
yet.

So the stage is now the top-level choice, and it changes the *structure* rather than
just the contents. Everything below is in the prototype.

---

## Stage 1 — First call

**Fifteen to twenty minutes, often on a phone, minutes before the call.**

Seven blocks, numbered, each with a minute mark and three or four scannable lines.
The first read is the headings and the opening card and takes three minutes. The
second read is every block and takes seventeen. The third is behind a disclosure and
nobody does it before a call.

### A prep surface, ahead of the dossier

- **What.** A new first tab. One screen that opens with who the call is with, the
  sentence to open on, how sure we are, and the line that governs everything under
  it: no ERP data yet, so every figure is modelled.
- **Why.** Every fact needed for a first call already existed and every one was on a
  different screen. Nobody assembles a briefing out of five surfaces with four
  minutes left; they read whatever they land on and go in with that.
- **How.** Nothing is authored twice. Every line is read off the same data the owning
  surface renders, so a prep card cannot drift from the dossier it summarises.

### Business context at a glance

- **What.** Seventeen facts in four groups: what they earn, who they sell to, who
  they buy from, how big the operation is. Revenue, EBITDA against the sector,
  profit, customers, suppliers, plants, invoice volume. Four show on the card; the
  rest are one click down.
- **Why.** The old version was six facts with no sources, no comparators and no
  profit line. It said what the company was, not what to make of it.
- **How.** Every fact carries what it should be measured against, a chip when the
  number is ours rather than theirs, and its source as a chip that opens the evidence
  panel.

### Signals of interest, and where the spend would go

- **What.** Two blocks. One reads signals off the company: a budget holder nine
  months into the job, an ERP decision that has to be made before 2027, what they are
  hiring for. The other says where the money would go — ₹6.2 Cr of the ₹9.7 Cr sits
  on work no system does today, biggest first.
- **Why.** "Why now" and "what would they actually buy" are the two questions a
  consultant is asked on every call and has been answering from memory.
- **How.** Both are derived, not written. The spend view is the estate with the money
  attached, so it cannot disagree with the findings list. Neither carries a total:
  the money belongs where it can show its base.

### Stakeholder-aware, by role and by domain

- **What.** Prep is anchored on the person you are meeting: their opening line, what
  not to raise, what they own, their share of the findings, what they are measured
  on. Questions can be arranged by person, and any view can be pointed at one part of
  the business.
- **Why.** The same twelve findings mean different things to a Head of Procurement
  and a CFO, and leading with the wrong one tells them the research was not about
  their function.
- **How.** The role decides the order and the wording, never the facts. Nothing is
  hidden from a role, it is sequenced for them.

### The stack, and who else is in the room

- **What.** Three lines: what is live, what is worked around, what was never bought.
  Each system names its supplier, and the block names the incumbent — TCS, on the SAP
  application management contract — with the sentence to answer it.
- **Why.** "What do you run on" is followed within a sentence by "who supplies it",
  and the answer decides who else has a veto on this deal.
- **How.** Every finding is filed under exactly one system, so the counts and the
  money under each reconcile to the same total as every other screen.

### The likeliest problems, ordered by how sure we are

- **What.** Three findings at rest, confirmed before inferred, with the tier word on
  every row. The drill-down names what has not been looked at at all.
- **Why.** Sorting by size puts the ₹2.1 Cr finding first, and that one rests on a
  single remark in a single call. Saying it out loud first is how a consultant loses
  the room.
- **How.** A sort, not a filter, so nothing disappears; the order is legible because
  the reason for it is on every row.

## Stage 2 — Second call

**An hour in the diary, read while it runs.**

Deliberately not the same screen. Fewer subjects, far more on each, and no minute
marks: a booked hour does not need a budget. The openers have been answered, so the
job has changed from learning who they are to testing what we think we heard.

### A verify list instead of a summary

- **What.** Four findings, each with the mechanism we think is behind it, what is
  still unknown, what to do next, and how many sources it rests on.
- **Why.** By the second call the finding is not news. What is worth an hour is the
  part we cannot yet defend, and a gap with no open questions is either finished or
  overclaimed.
- **How.** The same findings as the first-call block, one level deeper: the
  hypothesis and the open question are the content rather than the price.

### Question flows with first, second and third follow-ups

- **What.** Every question sits at one of three tiers. A broad opener, the follow-up
  that splits whatever came back, then the drill-downs that hang off each branch.
  Each question below the opener states the answer that sends you there: *if he says
  suppliers, or anything about getting them set up*.
- **Why.** A flat list says nothing about what to ask first and nothing at all about
  what to ask once the first one is answered. That is how a call opens on "what is
  your first-time match rate" before the other person has said what they care about.
- **How.** Three tiers and no more; a fourth is a script rather than a conversation.
  The build fails if a follow-up has no condition, if a drill-down hangs off an
  opener with the middle question missing, or if an opener has nothing under it.

### Technical questions kept apart from business ones

- **What.** Two columns on the second-call view, and a filter on Questions. Business
  impact asks what it costs them; technical asks how it works.
- **Why.** They are usually questions for different people, and three technical ones
  in a row is how a discovery call turns into a systems interview and the person who
  owns the problem stops recognising it as being about him.
- **How.** A third axis, independent of tier and domain: a technical question can be
  an opener and a business question can be a drill-down.

### Questions grouped by domain and tag, with filters

- **What.** Every question carries one domain (procurement, accounts payable,
  warehouse, finance, logistics, production) and tags from a closed list (SAP, master
  data, 3-way match, contracts, working capital, freight, yield, data request). Two
  dropdowns filter on them, a third on business against technical.
- **Why.** "Show me the SAP questions" is a real request and a domain cannot answer
  it: SAP runs through three of them. Domain is where a question sits; a tag is what
  it touches.
- **How.** Tags are chips on the rows, so the filter has a vocabulary the reader can
  learn. Filtering the tree keeps the route to every match, or a drill-down would
  appear with the two questions that earn it removed.

### The estate with vendors, not just modules

- **What.** A table of every system, who supplies it, and its state, with the
  incumbent risk and its counter underneath.
- **Why.** Scoping on a second call is a conversation about what we would be building
  on, and the answer includes the partner who runs it today.
- **How.** Where nobody supplies it, the row says what absorbs the work instead. The
  build fails on a system marked absent with nobody named.

## Stage 3 — Expansion

**Months later, and not before a call at all.**

Every block here is an argument from absence, which is why it is its own stage rather
than a longer findings list. None of it needs a meeting to be true.

### Gaps and the canvas, used for growth

- **What.** Three things: the stages nobody has researched with what is not being
  claimed for them, the nine level-2 processes on the map with nothing behind them,
  and the findings that were never in the first wave of the plan.
- **Why.** The canvas has been a picture of the first engagement. Its real value is
  later: a process with no evidence is a place the next piece of work could start,
  and that is exactly what an expansion conversation is short of.
- **How.** Findings after the first wave were not dropped, they were waiting on
  something. By expansion time that something has usually landed, and the block says
  what each one was waiting for.

### Overlap with past projects, as social proof

- **What.** A third tab on Compare, and it opens there. One line and one bar: we have
  built something like seven of these twelve before, worth ₹5.6 Cr of the ₹9.7 Cr,
  split four ways into built before, built but needs adapting, part of it built, and
  new build.
- **Why.** Time and Workflow both compare processes. Neither answers the question
  people actually arrive with, which is how much of this we have already done and for
  whom. On an expansion conversation it is the strongest sentence available.
- **How.** Every claim names the project, what was built there, what it took in weeks
  against our estimate here, and what was different. Three kinds of match, not two,
  because "we have done this" and "we have done something like it" are different
  promises and the second is the one that goes wrong in delivery. New build is a
  labelled band, not an absence: five findings, ₹4.1 Cr, where the estimate is
  softest.

### Match data that says how old it is

- **What.** Every past-project match carries the date it was last checked. Past six
  months the row says so itself, in the caveat colour, and every row has a *this
  match is wrong* control beside it.
- **Why.** A reuse claim ages badly and silently: the project we point at keeps
  running after we stop looking, the finding gets re-scoped, and the row goes on
  saying "built before" in the same confident voice it had in March.
- **How.** The control opens the correction prompt with the match attached; nobody
  retypes what was built at another client from this screen. The build fails on a
  match with no date.

## Changes that cross all three stages

### Restructure my view: focus on one domain

- **What.** A focus control at the top of Prep. Set it to procurement and the
  findings, the systems and the questions on every stage are the ones procurement
  owns.
- **Why.** "Let me look at just this part of the business" is one instruction, and
  answering it means filtering three things keyed three different ways.
- **How.** It walks links that already exist: a domain's questions name the findings
  they test, and every finding sits under exactly one system. No new taxonomy, so
  nothing to keep in step.

### Less on top, more one click down

- **What.** Every block shows three or four lines and names what is behind its
  disclosure: *the other thirteen facts*, not *more*. On the map, a process opens its
  detail on a click and drills a level on a double-click. In the dossier, sections
  open one at a time.
- **Why.** "More" tells a reader nothing about whether it is worth the click, which
  is the whole decision they are making.
- **How.** Drill-downs mount rather than hide, so a closed block keeps its links out
  of the tab order and its text out of the page for a screen reader.

### Ask Helix: chat and search over the research

- **What.** A side panel from the masthead. Ask a direct question and get an answer
  with its sources attached; select any text or open any process and *Ask Helix*
  attaches it as the subject.
- **Why.** Half of what a consultant wants is one fact, and finding it means knowing
  which of six surfaces owns it.
- **How.** Attaching is not asking. The subject arrives on the composer as a chip and
  the caret lands in the box; the question stays the user's to write. It answers only
  from the loaded research or says it has nothing.

### Transcripts, and what came out of them

- **What.** Every source row says what was drawn from it in words: *3 findings ·
  5 claims · 4 signals · 2 risks*, or *read, nothing drawn from it*. Opening one shows
  the quoted line behind every finding it produced.
- **Why.** A single citation count cannot tell a transcript that produced four
  findings from one that merely corroborated four somebody else made, and those are
  opposite readings of how useful the document was.
- **How.** A finding counts once per source, not once per quote. Sources that carry
  nothing are a true state and stay on the list saying so.

### Capturing a gap the research missed

- **What.** Add a finding from the Gaps surface, or edit one, in a drawer with the
  fields a finding cannot exist without.
- **Why.** A gap heard on a call that the research never saw is a new observation,
  not a rewrite of model output.
- **How.** There is no price field: a number typed into a box has no base, no rate
  and no range. Why we believe it, the confidence and the evidence are shown but not
  editable, because those change when a source changes and not when an opinion does.
  The form ends by stating what would be recorded: who added it, and when.

### Correction by prompt, never by retyping

- **What.** Everywhere something can be wrong — a process on the map, a finding, a
  past-project match — the control describes the correction to the assistant rather
  than opening an edit field.
- **Why.** A hand-corrected claim loses its provenance, and the chain from source to
  claim to price stops being walkable.
- **How.** One route, one audit trail. A flagged item carries the reason in words,
  and the build fails on a flag with no reason.

## Where each piece landed

| Surface | What changed | Density |
|---|---|---|
| **Prep** *(new)* | Three stages, seven first-call blocks, focus control | 448 words · 1.5 screens |
| **Questions** | Conversation tree, domain and tag grouping, business against technical, three filters | 666 words |
| **Compare** | Built before: overlap, social proof, match freshness, correction route | 728 words |
| **Research** | Business context with comparators and sources, on About and Money | 17 facts |
| **Sources** | What each document produced, broken down by kind | 9 sources |
| **Gaps** | Add and edit a finding; plan unchanged | 140 words |
| **Operations** | Feeds the expansion stage: processes with nothing behind them | 9 blank |

**One fix worth naming.** Every folded section in the dossier needed two clicks to
open: the toggle read a stored value that did not exist yet, so the first press wrote
"closed" over "closed". It had been that way since sections started shipping folded.

## What this does not add

No client-facing presentation mode. No manual editing of model output. No new data
behind any of it: every number on every new screen is read off research that already
existed, which is what keeps a prep card from disagreeing with the dossier it
summarises. Where a screen needed a judgement the data does not carry, the judgement
is a sort or a filter, never a new sentence somebody has to maintain.
